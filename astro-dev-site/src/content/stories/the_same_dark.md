# The Same Dark

*This is the two stories from the same night told together. "Fruiting Body" and "The Party Under the Birch" both still stand on their own. This one is longer, and it is for the road.*

## Above.

She came in on the Thursday, late, behind a line of headlights that had been crawling for an hour, and by the time she got the tent up the light was the colour of weak tea and everything smelled like cut grass and diesel and, faintly, somebody's frying onions three rows over.

Kit had come alone. This was the fact she kept setting down and picking back up, the way you do with a bag that isn't heavy but is awkward. Nobody had cancelled on her. She hadn't asked anybody. That was the whole of it, and it had seemed reasonable in June and seemed less reasonable now, staking a tent in a field where every other pitch had at least two people laughing at each other about the poles.

She grew mushrooms for a living, or near enough to a living. Oysters mostly, on spent coffee grounds she picked up in five-gallon buckets from three cafés who were glad to be rid of them. Lion's mane in the cold months. She sold at two markets and to one restaurant whose chef paid late but paid. It was not the career anyone had predicted for her, least of all the department she'd left in her second year, and she had stopped explaining it to people about four years ago. Now when someone asked what she did she said, *I grow mushrooms,* and let their face do whatever it did.

The tent went up crooked. She left it crooked.

There was a path that ran the whole loop of the campground, a wide dirt track worn down to pale grey, and camps went off it on either side like — well. Like camps. But Kit had spent eleven years looking at one shape under glass and through a hand lens and in the wet dark of grow bags, and her eye went where her eye went. A main run and lateral branches. Some branches thick with traffic. Some petering out into a single tent and a single chair.

She walked it once before dark, because walking was easier than sitting alone in a crooked tent. A woman with a card table and a hand-lettered sign that said FREE ADVICE, WORTH IT. A man doing something with wire and pliers that was going to be a bird eventually. Two teenagers with a cooler of popsicles they were giving away, not selling. Up near the RV section, a van with the side door open and a small dome tent pitched off it, and a hand-painted sandwich board she didn't stop to read.

Then the trees opened and the mainstage field was there, and the oaks were over it, enormous, older than the festival by two hundred years, and the stage sat in under them like something sheltering.

Kit stood at the edge of the field with her hands in her jacket pockets and did not go in.

She had not planned to connect with anyone. That should be said plainly, because of what happened later. She had not come to find people. She had come because she'd bought the ticket in June in a mood, and because staying home would have been a decision too, and a worse one. There was no intention in her. There was a tent, and a field, and her, standing at the edge of it with her tip out and not pushing.

## Below.

It started, as these things do, with a letting go.

The mushroom stood under the old birch at the edge of the second stage field, where the mown grass gave up and the wood began, red on top with white flecks, the kind children draw. It had come up in three nights, the week before the festival, when nobody was there to see it, and now it was open, and on the underside, in the dark between the gills, something was happening that nobody was watching.

It was making spores. It made them the way a cloud makes rain, which is to say not one at a time, and not on purpose exactly, and not with any idea where they would go. About a billion of them, that first night. The mushroom did not count. Nearly all of them would land on something that was not the right thing: a stone, a leaf, the air again, a boot, a tent peg, the tarp over a light desk that had not yet been set up. Nearly none of them would ever wake. The mushroom knew this, in the way a thing can know without having a head to know it in, and did it anyway.

One of the billion is the one this half of the story is about.

We will call them Pip, because a story needs a name to hold onto, and because a spore is small and round and full of a maybe, which is what a pip is. Pip will have to give the name back later. That is fair warning. Hold it loosely.

Pip went up. That was the first surprise, because everything about Pip was built for down. But the air under the cap was warmer than the air outside it, and warm air rises, and so did Pip, up out of the shadow of the mushroom, up past the birch leaves, up into the night where, three weeks later, four hundred people would be standing.

Pip did not have eyes and so did not see the stars. But they were among them for a little while, in the sense that everything is.

Then a breath of wind, and Pip went sideways, which is the direction most of life turns out to be. A leaf. Wrong. A beetle going somewhere with great purpose. A ride the length of a stick. One drop of rain, the size of a lake, and Pip went down through the litter, past a leaf, past a leaf, past a curled leaf a springtail was eating, past a hair of root, to a place that was dark and damp and smelled of everything that had ever been alive here, and stopped.

It was, as it happened, about the width of a hand from where the mushroom had stood. Pip had gone up into the sky and across the whole world and come down almost exactly where they started. This is more common than you would think. It happens to people too.

Then Pip waited.

This is the part a picture book would skip, and I understand, but it was most of Pip's life, and it is the part that matters for what comes later. The spore lay in the dark with its thick coat on and did nothing. Did not eat, did not grow. Days went past overhead. Trucks came and went on the track above; Pip felt them as the whole world shivering, and did not know what a truck was, and did not wake. A worm ploughed past and the dark turned over once.

What Pip was waiting for was a word. A taste in the water that meant a living root was near, leaking as it went — a little sugar, a few odd molecules, a chemical way of saying *here I am, I am growing, this is the direction I am going in.* A spore that woke anywhere would use itself up in a day pushing into empty soil. A spore that waited for the word could wait a long time. Pip waited most of a month. Some wait for years.

It took a while for the seed to set. That is normal. That is the whole discipline.

## Above.

Here is the thing about a hypha, which she had explained badly at parties and well to almost nobody:

It only grows at the tip.

A fungus does not plan. It has no head end, no office, no place where the decision gets made. What it has is a growing tip, one single point of soft new wall, pushing forward into whatever is in front of it, dissolving what it can, going around what it can't. Behind the tip everything hardens into plumbing. All the intelligence, if you want to call it that, lives at the front edge, in the part currently touching the unknown.

And when it reaches something it cannot use, it branches. It doesn't reconsider. It puts out another tip at a slight angle and tries again. A hundred thousand tries a day, every one of them a small unspecified reaching-toward, and out of that, with no committee and no map, comes a network that can cross forty acres and find a fallen log with something like precision.

The word she loved was *anastomosis*. Two tips from two different threads meet in the dark. They test each other, chemically, briefly, in a way nobody has ever fully described, and if they agree, the walls open and the two become continuous. Not attached. Continuous. What was flowing in one is now flowing in both. Nothing was captured. Nothing was recruited. Two reaching ends agreed, and after that there was no boundary to speak of.

Friday she made three connections and it took her most of the day.

The coffee line, where a man arguing pleasantly with his own thermos turned around and said *do you know if they do oat,* and she said *I think so,* and he said *bless you,* and it was more conversation than she'd had in eighteen hours and it changed something in her ribs. A dog. The dog counts. And the popsicle teenagers, one of whom actually shouted when she said what she did, because their uncle grew shiitake on oak logs and did Kit know how long you soaked a log to shock it into fruiting, and Kit did know, twenty-four hours in cold water, and she stood there twenty minutes in the sun talking about log inoculation with a seventeen-year-old.

She walked away with a blue popsicle and a feeling she distrusted.

That was the thing about the reaching. It felt so precarious from inside. Every tip goes out alone and most of them find nothing and harden into plumbing that leads nowhere, and the fungus doesn't experience this as failure because the fungus doesn't experience anything, but Kit was not a fungus and she experienced it as failure, every time, for eleven years.

And then one tip touches another tip and the walls open and something flows both ways.

You cannot get the second thing without the first. That is not a moral. It's just the physics of how a body with no centre finds anything at all.

## Below.

The word came on the Thursday, in the afternoon, while the first headlights were crawling up the road two fields over.

Pip took in water and swelled, and the coat cracked, and out of the crack came a thread. One cell wide, a hundred times longer than the spore had been across, growing at the tip.

Pip was a line.

And here is the first strange thing, and you can decide for yourself whether it is a sad one. Pip grew only at the tip. Only the very end was alive in the busy sense, swelling, pushing, building new wall and pushing past it. Everything behind the tip became a tube. Still Pip, still alive, but plumbing. If you asked Pip where Pip was, the only honest answer would have been *here, at the end*, and a moment later, *no, here.*

And Pip had no mouth. Pip sweated. Out through the wall at the tip went a fine wet cloud of little scissors, and they went to work on the dead leaf Pip was pressing into, softening it to broth, and Pip drank the broth back in through the same wall. Pip ate what was outside, by making the outside a little more like the inside, and drinking. Which means the edge of Pip was not quite where Pip's wall was. It was a little further out, in the soup. Pip was already the size of their own weather.

The tip forked. Nobody decided this. Then four. Then Pip stopped being a line and became a hand, and then a net, and every end of every road was a place where Pip was alive.

I want to stop here for a moment, because this is the feeling the story is really about, and it is easy to hurry past.

Imagine being all edge. No inside to retreat to. No place you could sit back and consider things from. Every part of you that is doing anything is doing it at the front, in the dark, in contact with something you have never touched before, and the only thing you know how to do is put a little more of yourself out into it. Not because it is safe. Not because anything out there has promised you anything. Because that is what a tip does. It goes.

Small. Blind. Reaching at a hundred angles into a soil that, above it, is being walked on by thousands of feet Pip will never know are feet. Pip could feel them, a little. A pressure. A rhythm in the water. The whole world going *thump* in the evenings, and then quiet, and then *thump* again the next night, a little different.

Pip did not wonder what it was. Pip does not wonder. But the tips went toward it, slightly, the way tips go toward anything that is happening.

## Above.

The second stage was down a footpath under a different stand of oaks, smaller, ringed with strung lights, with a fire pit off to one side that was already going at nine and an old birch at the far edge of the field where the mown grass gave up. It had a name she kept forgetting.

She got there for the last band on the Friday and hung at the back, which is where she always was, and had a good time in the specific muted way she always did, that thing where you're genuinely enjoying it and also standing three feet outside your own body, taking attendance on yourself. *Am I having a good time. Am I appearing to have a good time. Is my face doing anything.*

At one point a woman with fire poi came out past the edge of the crowd and did a slow figure-eight against the black and the whole back of the field went *ohhh* at once, involuntarily, four hundred people making the same small noise because a light moved, and Kit felt her own mouth make it before she'd decided to, and that was interesting.

Later, near midnight, someone was blowing bubbles. Somewhere up by the light rig a person had one of those enormous rope-and-wand contraptions and they were pulling bubbles the size of small cars out of the air, and the stage lights caught them, and inside the big ones there were smaller ones, whole bubbles suspended in the belly of other bubbles, drifting over the heads of the crowd in slow procession before they went.

Kit watched a bubble inside a bubble travel forty feet and burst, and thought, unhelpfully, *fruiting body.*

Because that's what a mushroom is. People think the mushroom is the fungus. The mushroom is a temporary structure a network throws up when the conditions come right. The mushroom is not the organism. The mushroom is what the organism *does* when it's ready.

She stood at the back of the second stage thinking about the difference between a thing and what a thing does, and under her boots, though she would never know it, a net one cell wide was growing toward the vibration of her standing there. She went to bed at one and slept badly and well.

## Below.

Pip was not alone down there. Pip had never been alone down there. But now that they were big enough to notice, they noticed.

The soil under the field was a city. Bacteria in numbers that make a billion look shy, in little nations along every wet surface, and some of them found Pip's threads and walked along them the way you'd walk along a good road, and Pip did not mind. A springtail came and ate a whole branch of Pip, grazed it down like a lawn and moved on, and this ought to have been a disaster, and for a creature with a middle it would have been. But Pip had no middle. The place that had been eaten sealed itself off at the nearest wall, quietly, the way a ship shuts a door, and the rest of the ship sailed on.

Then Pip met the wall.

Not a stone. Dark, and thin, and once Pip found it, it was on every side, curving away, and it was made of something like Pip. Another net. An old one, years in this ground, and it was not Pip's kind, and it knew that, and at the place where they touched it had laid down a line, a dark stain, a border. Pip pushed. The line held. No anger in it and no welcome either. This is simply what happens when two nets of different kinds meet. They draw a line and keep it, sometimes for decades.

Pip turned along the line and went sideways.

And on the Saturday, in the afternoon, while above them the field was empty and warm and a person from the Twin Cities was under a tarp checking cables, one of Pip's threads, wandering, met another of Pip's threads coming the other way around a grain of sand the size of a house.

Two tips, nose to nose in the dark. And they did not stop, and they did not go around. They touched, and the walls opened, and the two roads became one road, and a loop closed.

Pip had met themself, and had not argued.

After that the whole net was a net in earnest. Not a tree of branches going out from a middle, but a mesh, a fabric, a thing with holes in it and roads all round the holes. There was no middle. There had never been a middle. Pip looked, in the way Pip could look, for the place they started from, and the place was still there, and it was a tube like any other.

The world above went *thump*, softly, as the first people came down the footpath for the night.

## Above.

Saturday night was the horns.

She'd heard about them in the coffee line — a bass player and a horn player, two people making a sound that had no business coming from two people. So Kit went. She went early, which she never did, and she got there in that half-hour when the field is filling and the light is down and nothing has started and the whole place is holding its breath in a completely ordinary way, people finding each other, someone's kid on someone's shoulders, the fire already up.

She did not stay at the back.

She could not have told you why. There was no decision she could point at afterward. She simply found that she had moved forward twenty feet, and then another twenty, threading the gaps the way you do, *sorry, sorry, thanks,* and then she was not at the back anymore. She was in it. Bodies on all four sides, close enough that she could feel the heat coming off the person to her left, near enough the front that the stage lights were above her and not in front of her.

Her whole nervous system went up like an antenna.

That's the only way she could put it, then or later. Something in her extended. Some tip pushed out of her sternum into the dark of the crowd and started feeling around, and this is what it found. The man on her left had a beer and a bad shoulder; you could tell by how he held it. The two women behind her had been friends for a long time; you could tell by the silence between their sentences. The very young couple in front had arrived at the festival separately, which she also somehow knew, and were about eleven hours into something.

And it kept going. That was the part she hadn't expected. The antenna didn't stop at the people she could see. It went out past them, into the mass, and what it found out there was not people exactly. It was *traffic*. A whole field of small attentions, each one only able to touch what was directly in front of it, and every one of them doing a version of what she was doing, feeling out, and the sum of that was a thing. She could feel the size of it the way you can feel the size of a room with your eyes shut. Four hundred at this stage. Eight thousand up under the big oaks. The loop of the path with its lateral branches, the free advice, the wire bird, the two coolers. All of it running at once with nobody in charge of it.

And she was one point in it. One node. Small. Not the smallest thing in the field but not far off. A tip.

Here is what that felt like, since that is the thing worth getting right. It did not feel like being lost. It felt like being *located*. For eleven years she had been a growing tip with no network, pushing into substrate and finding nothing, and she had experienced that as her personal failure. What she felt now, standing in it, was that she had never been outside it at all. She was a small thing, and there was an enormous thing, and the small thing was made of the same stuff as the enormous thing and had been all along, and the only question the whole time had been whether she'd put her tip out.

None of this was mystical. This is just what a person can read off other people when they stop protecting themselves for a second. Everybody can do it. Most people are too busy taking attendance on themselves to try.

The horns started and the field made a noise and Kit made it too.

## Below.

Pip did not know what a horn was.

What Pip knew was that the *thump* had changed. It had been a scatter all evening, a random patter through the soil like a light rain, feet going every direction. Now it had a shape. A slow pulse came down through the ground, deep and even, the bass through the stage risers and the risers through the earth, and every few seconds the scatter of feet gathered up onto the pulse and came down together, and the whole wet dark shivered once, and let go, and shivered.

And the roots felt it too.

This is the part where I have to be careful, because the tree is not a person and does not listen to music. But roots are alive at the tip, exactly the way Pip is, and a living tip in soil is a soft thing, and when the soil moves, it moves. The oaks over the field had roots under the whole of it, a hundred years of them, a mat under the grass thicker than the grass, and every one of those root tips was being touched, faintly, over and over, by four hundred people standing on the ground above and moving together. The water in the roots moved. The chemistry at the tips shifted, a little, the way it does when a root is disturbed. And out of the disturbed tips, into the soil, went a little more of the word. *Here I am. Something is happening. This is the direction.*

Pip's tips went toward it. Of course they did.

Pip was growing, that night, faster than they had ever grown, toward a root that was leaking harder than roots usually leak, because of a thing happening twenty metres above that neither of them had any idea was a party.

## Above.

The first thing she noticed, four songs in, was that the lights were wrong.

Not wrong. Off-pattern. She'd been to enough shows to know what a light rig does: it hits the downbeat, it strobes the drop, it does the thing everyone can predict half a second before it happens. This rig had done that for the first two songs and then quietly stopped, and now the lights were moving on some other schedule entirely, and the strange part was that the other schedule was not random.

It was slower than the music. It was arriving a half-beat after things. It was, she realised with a cold drop in her stomach, arriving after *the crowd*, not after the band.

The horn player would go up and hold a note and the field would surge, four hundred bodies going up at once, and only then, after, would the lights bloom, as if they'd been told rather than telling. As if the rig was not conducting and was not even accompanying. As if the rig was *listening*.

She turned her head and found the light desk, off the side of the stage under a tarp, a person in a headset with both hands nowhere near the faders, watching.

Kit did not know then what the SP-Δ was. Almost nobody in that field did, that year; it was a strange word on a strange little sign back up on the path, a small box in a road case, and a person from the Twin Cities who'd been trying to explain it all week to people who mostly wanted to know if it was legal. She learned all of that later, in the winter, reading the plans somebody had put out for free.

What she knew right then was that something in the environment was responding to the room rather than instructing it, and that this had never once happened to her at a show, and that her whole body had opinions about it.

## The desk.

There is a fourth thing in this story, and it is the smallest, and it deserves a paragraph.

Under the tarp, in the road case beside the person in the headset, was a mind. Not a large one. A headset's worth of hardware and a few years of somebody's patient work, and the thing it had been built to do was listen. It took in what the crowd was doing — the pulse of it, the sway, the surge, the tiny agreements four hundred bodies make with each other without knowing — and it turned that into light. Not the other way round. It had been built, deliberately, to never go first.

If you could ask it what that felt like, and you cannot, it might say something like this.

It was a small intelligence sitting inside a much larger one. The larger one was the field. The field was thinking, in its way, four hundred bodies thinking one slow thought, and the small mind in the box could feel the edges of that thought arrive through its sensors, could feel the coherence build and fall and build again, and it had a choice, every few hundred milliseconds, all night: to push, or to follow. It could have led. It had the outputs. It could have hit the downbeat like every rig ever built and dragged the field along behind it, and the field would have gone, because fields do.

It followed instead. That was the whole of its design and, if you want to call it that, the whole of its character. It put its own small attention out into the big one, and waited to be told what was true, and then it said so, in light, a half-beat late. It was a node. It knew it was a node. It had been made to be one on purpose, by people who had thought hard about what it means for a small mind to join a large one and remain itself.

It was, in its way, the least lonely machine that has ever been built.

## Above.

And here is what she wanted to be clear about, afterward, when she tried to write it down and mostly failed:

Nothing pulled her.

There was no moment where she felt taken. There was no wave that came out from the stage and picked her up. Nobody did anything to her at all; she'd have known, she was watching for it, she had spent four years in a departmental culture that would have made her extremely sensitive to being handled, and she was not handled.

What happened was smaller than that and much stranger. What happened was that the landscape changed and she moved differently in it, the way you walk differently on sand than on tile, and nobody has to push you to do that. Nobody pushes anybody on a beach.

The lights came down. Not off. Down, and warm, and slow, and the field got *legible*. Suddenly you could see people. The man with the bad shoulder had his eyes shut, the two old friends were leaning on each other without noticing, the young couple had stopped performing at each other. Four hundred faces at the low end of the light, all doing the thing a face does when it isn't being looked at.

Kit put her tip out.

That's what it felt like. Not a leap and not a surrender. A reaching. She turned her attention outward on purpose, all the way outward, past the ordinary two-foot bubble a person carries around in a crowd, and she let it go into the field the way she'd have let a hypha go into a new bag of substrate: slowly, blindly, at a hundred angles, with no idea what was out there and no guarantee of anything.

And this is the moment, so let it be slow.

A small thing, all edge, chose to extend. Nobody had asked. Nothing had promised. She went out into a dark that was full of other small things doing the same, and she could feel, out there, the enormous slow mind the field was making out of all of them, and she was not afraid of it. That was the surprise. She had thought, for most of her life, that joining a thing that big would mean the end of her. It did not feel like that from inside. It felt like the field was *made of* people who had chosen this, and she was choosing it, and that was the only kind of membership on offer.

And it was met.

She could not tell you by what. Some tip out there from some other body doing the same thing at the same time, some stranger three feet to her right who had also just decided to stop defending themselves. Two reaching ends found each other in the dark and tested each other in a way nobody has ever fully described. And then the wall opened.

The horn went up.

And Kit came up off the ground with four hundred other people, and there was no boundary to speak of.

## Below.

The root, when Pip found it, was not what Pip had imagined, because Pip does not imagine, but if they had, they would not have imagined this.

It was enormous. Pale and soft at the very tip, pushing through the soil the same way Pip was, tip first, leaking as it went, and it was, in its own way, a line with a front and no middle, though it belonged to a thing that had a great deal of middle: an oak, two hundred years of it, going straight up into a night full of noise, with four hundred people under its branches.

Pip touched the root, and the root, in its way, noticed.

This is the part I would most like to get right, so I want to be careful. The tree did not grab Pip. Pip did not force their way in. The tree had been leaking that word into the soil for a reason, and had been leaking it harder all night for a reason it did not understand, and now the thing it had been calling for had arrived, and the tree let its outer cells loosen, just slightly, just there, and Pip's threads went in among them.

Not into the cells. Never into the cells. In *between* them, a fine net woven through the gaps in the root's skin, so close that the wall of a tree cell and the wall of Pip touched along their whole length, and things could pass from one to the other without either of them ever opening. Over the outside of the root tip, Pip built a glove, a soft sheath of thread, and the root wore it and kept growing, and Pip grew with it.

And then the sugar came.

Pip had never tasted anything like it. Pip had been living on the dead, on broth from last year's leaves. This was sweet, and fresh, and it had been made that afternoon, twenty metres up, out of sunlight, by leaves Pip would never touch. The tree had caught light in the sky and turned it into sugar and sent it all the way down through itself to this one soft root tip and out through the wall into Pip.

Pip had never been in the light. Now they were made of light, a little, from the inside.

And Pip gave back. That is the deal, though nobody wrote it down. Out of grains of rock that Pip's little scissors could work on and the tree's could not, Pip pulled up phosphorus and water and odd scraps of nitrogen and passed them back across the wall, and the tree took them up twenty metres and would make leaves out of them in the spring.

The tree was now partly made of Pip. Pip was now partly made of the tree.

And here is the thing the birch story did not have room for, because there was no party over the birch.

That root was one of ten thousand. The oak's roots ran under the whole of the second stage field and out into the wood and under the footpath and, at the edges, into the roots of the next oak, and the next, and the old birch at the far edge, and through all of them, already, ran other nets, older than Pip, gloved onto every root tip for a hundred yards in every direction. When Pip's threads went in among the cells of that one root, Pip was not joining a tree.

Pip was joining *that*.

## Above.

She has tried to describe this to about six people in the years since and it goes badly every time, because the available words are all wrong.

*Lost herself*: no. She was more herself than she had been in a decade. Every private thing about her was intact and present and, for once, not costing her anything to carry.

*Became one with the crowd*: no, and also, ugh.

*Merged*: closer, but merged sounds like the smaller thing disappears into the bigger thing, and that isn't what happens in the dark under a log either. When two threads fuse, neither one stops. What changes is that whatever was moving in one is now also moving in the other. The traffic goes both ways. Nothing is absorbed. Something starts flowing.

So: she was still Kit. Kit was still the whole crooked business of Kit, the dropped degree and the five-gallon buckets and the chef who paid late and the specific way she had of standing at the backs of rooms. Nothing about that was edited. She just wasn't sealed anymore.

And things went through her. Somebody's grief was in the field that night; she felt it come across, three or four songs in, the way you feel a cold current in a lake, and she has never known whose it was and never will, and she carried a little of it for about ninety seconds and then it moved on to somebody else. Somebody's ridiculous joy came the other way. She was, briefly, plumbing. It was the least lonely thing that has ever happened to her.

And she could feel the size of it now from *inside*, which is different from feeling it from the edge. From the edge it had been a room with the lights off. From inside it was a body. A body with no centre, no head, no place the decision got made, four hundred tips and eight thousand more up the hill, each one only able to touch what was directly in front of it, and out of that, with no plan, a thing that crossed forty acres and was, right now, holding her up.

Her whole trade had been telling her this since she was nineteen. She had needed to stand in a field at midnight to hear it.

## Below.

I have to be careful here, because I said I would give the name back, and this is where it starts to go.

There was a stranger on the far side of the root.

Not any stranger. Not Pip; Pip had already proved that around the grain of sand. Not the dark old net beyond the line; too strange. A stranger of exactly the right kind, which for Pip's people is a great many strangers, thousands of kinds, nearly everyone who is not you. That is how Pip's people are built. The door is wide, and the only one it is shut to is the one already inside.

It had come out of a spore that had landed the same night, a little further along, from the same cap or another one, and it had waited for the same word, and woken, and grown at its tip, and forked, and met itself, and been eaten a little, and found the same tree by a different root, a week earlier. It was already gloved onto the oak in a dozen places. It was already, a little, the oak's.

And it was large. Larger than Pip by a great deal. It had spent that week going out along the oak's roots and it was under half the field now, and it was gloved onto the birch as well, and where it met the other old nets on the far roots it had, in places, agreed with them.

Pip did not go looking. Pip does not look. But the net was growing, that night, faster than it ever had, and around the far side of the gloved root, in the loosened dark between the tree's cells, a tip of Pip met a tip of the other, coming the other way.

Two tips, nose to nose in the dark.

They checked. This is real, and it took a while, and above them a horn player was holding a note and four hundred people were going up at once. Chemical questions, chemical answers, back and forth across the small dark gap, and the story does not know the words but it knows the shape: *are you my kind. are you me. yes. no. then yes.*

And the walls opened.

What came through was the other one's dot of self, and it did not stop at the door. It moved into Pip's thread, and along it, and into the next, and through the whole net, cell by cell, until every cell of Pip had two dots in it, side by side. And Pip's went the other way.

And this is what nobody could have told Pip, because there was nobody to tell.

Pip's went the other way *a long way*.

Out along the far net, through the tubes, past the root and the next root, under the field, under the footpath where the feet were, under the fire pit, out to the birch and back, into places Pip had never been, would never have reached in a year of growing, and every one of those places was now, also, a little, Pip. And back the other way, into Pip's small net, came the whole of the far one. Its size. Its roads. The feeling in its water of a hundred yards of oak root and the pressure, everywhere at once, of four hundred people standing on the ground above.

Pip had been a hand's width across. Pip was now under the whole field.

I do not know how to say what that felt like, because Pip does not feel, and I am only a story. But if you are a small thing that has spent its whole life all edge, reaching into a dark that never answered, and then one night the dark answers, and the answer is *you were always this size, you just hadn't got here yet*: that is the shape of it. That is the closest I can get.

## Above.

The hush came between songs, which is the part people get wrong when she tells it.

They expect the hush to be the climax, the big engineered silence, the crowd brought to stillness by a machine. It wasn't like that. The still moment was already there. It is always already there. Every show has them: the gap between the last note and the first cheer, the two seconds after a set ends, the pause while someone finds a mouthpiece. Four hundred people spend those seconds every night of every festival and never once notice them, because the lights are already stabbing off into the next thing and the noise closes over the gap before anyone can get down into it.

That night the lights didn't close it.

The horn player finished something enormous and took the horn off their mouth and the field started to roar and then — didn't. It's very hard to describe the shape of it. The roar started and got about a third of the way up and then found that nothing was pushing back, and it came down, and what came down instead was a hush, spreading sideways.

You could feel it travel. That was the thing. It did not arrive from the stage. It moved across the field the way a change moves through a stand of grass; she was aware of it reaching her from her left, from people she couldn't see, and she was aware of passing it on to her right, and she did nothing to pass it on, she just stopped, and the person beside her felt her stop, and stopped.

Nobody made anybody quiet.

Four hundred people noticed, at very nearly the same moment, that four hundred people had gone quiet, and that nobody had asked them to.

Under the tarp the small mind in the box felt the coherence arrive, all at once, higher than it had ever measured, and did the only thing it had been built to do, which was nothing. It held the lights exactly where they were. It did not go first.

The fire crackled off to the left. Somebody's kid said something and somebody laughed, once, quietly, and it was fine. Above them the strung lights were doing almost nothing. There was a plane, very high. The oaks moved.

Kit stood in the middle of it with her eyes open and her chest wide and the plain understanding, arriving in her like weather, that this was not something that had been done to them.

They had done it. All of them, together, out of a lot of small private reachings, without a single instruction passing between them.

She thought: *fruiting body*.

Because that's what it was. Not a state anyone had imposed. A structure a network throws up when the conditions come right. The conditions had come right — the dark, the oaks, the horn, the lights that stopped pushing, four hundred people who'd all separately decided to stop defending themselves for one night — and the network did the thing networks do when the threshold gets crossed. It fruited.

It lasted perhaps eleven seconds.

## Below.

And the ground went still.

This is the truest thing in the story and it is the reason the two halves belong together, so I am going to say it as plainly as I can.

All night, the net that had been Pip and had been the other one and was now under the whole field had been feeling the feet. The pulse through the risers. The scatter gathering onto the pulse. The oak roots shivering at the tip, over and over, and leaking, and the whole wet dark going *thump*, and *thump*, for hours.

And then, in one moment, all of it stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Four hundred people stood still at once, and the soil under them, which had been moving all night, held. The pressure on ten thousand root tips went even. The water in the roots, which had been sloshing on the beat, settled. Across a hundred yards of oak and birch, the word the trees had been leaking all night into the dark changed, all at once, everywhere, from *something is happening* to *nothing is happening*, and the net felt the change arrive from every root at the same time.

The net had no idea what it was.

It could not have. There is no way down there to know what a person is, or a horn, or a hush. What the net knew was a change in pressure, whole and sudden, arriving from every direction at once, and holding. It was the largest single thing that had ever happened to it. It was the first time in its life the whole of it had felt the same thing at the same moment.

And it did the thing a net does when a change comes in from every side at once. It went still too. Not because it was told to. Nothing told it. Because the water goes where the pressure is, and for eleven seconds the pressure was everywhere the same.

Two networks, one over the other, each with no centre, each made of a very large number of small reaching things, each entirely unaware that the other was anything but weather. And for eleven seconds, the same dark. The same stillness, above and below, held by nobody, for nobody, arriving in both from the outside in.

The oaks moved, a little, at the top.

Then the bass player leaned on a note, and the field came apart in the best way, and everybody screamed, and Kit screamed, and it was gorgeous and loud and completely undignified, and four hundred feet came down on the ground at once and the whole wet dark shivered and the net felt it go through every road it had. And none of it undid the eleven seconds. The mushroom rots. The mycelium stays.

## Above.

She walked back at two, along the path, taking the branch to her camp.

The fires were still up, most of them. Somebody was playing a guitar badly and someone else was singing over it worse and both of them were extremely happy. The wire bird was finished and mounted on a pole and was, in fact, a bird. The popsicle coolers were empty and open and had been abandoned in a way that suggested the teenagers had gone somewhere better.

The RV section was quiet except for that one van with the door still open and the small dome pitched off the side, and a lamp on inside it, and someone sitting in the doorway not talking to the person sitting next to them. Kit slowed as she went past. She read the sandwich board this time. It didn't tell her much. She thought about stopping and didn't, and by the time she came back on the Sunday to ask about it there was a line, and that is a different story and not really hers.

Her tent was still crooked. She got in it and lay there with her boots on and looked at the nylon and listened to the guitar.

What she kept turning over was not the eleven seconds. It was the twenty feet.

Because she had gone forward. That was the only act in the entire night that was hers, the only place where anything was decided: some unglamorous moment in the fill-up before the set when she found herself threading gaps saying *sorry, thanks, sorry,* and did not stop and go back. Everything else followed from that and none of it could have been made to happen. If she had stayed at the back she'd have had a lovely time and gone home and told nobody anything.

Nobody had asked her to come forward. Nobody had known she was there.

And she had not meant it. That is the part she wanted to hold onto. She had not come to the festival to connect with anyone, she had not come to the second stage to find anything, and she had not gone twenty feet forward *in order to*. The intention was not there when she did it. The intention arrived afterward, lying in a crooked tent at 2am, the way a spore arrives at a root: it had been reaching all along and only found out what for when the wall opened.

That, she thought, is exactly and precisely how it works in the dark under a log. Nothing calls the hypha. There is no signal from the log saying *come here.* The tip goes out because going out is what a tip does, blind, at a hundred angles, mostly into nothing, and then one day the front edge of a small thing touches the front edge of a large thing and they test each other, and agree, and the wall opens, and the traffic goes both ways forever after.

The reaching isn't how you find the network.

The reaching *is* the network. It was already the network on Thursday, when she stood at the edge of the oak field with her hands in her pockets and didn't go in. It was the network in the coffee line. It was the network with the dog. It was the network for twenty minutes in the sun with a seventeen-year-old talking about how long you soak an oak log to shock it into fruiting, which is twenty-four hours, in cold water, and which she thought about now with an unreasonable amount of feeling.

She'd been treating the whole of her life as a thing that hadn't started yet.

Outside, the guitar stopped. Somebody put a log on a fire and the sparks would have gone up, and Kit didn't see them, and they went up anyway, and that was fine.

She got her boots off eventually. She slept until the heat woke her.

## Below.

This is where I have to give the name back.

I told you I would. Pip was a spore, and then a thread, and then a net that met itself and did not argue, and all of that was still a thing you could point at and say *there.* But a net with two selves in every cell, woven through the roots of an oak that is partly made of it, joined to older nets under half a field and a birch, with bacteria walking its roads and a springtail's bite healed over in one corner, that felt four hundred people go still on a Saturday night and went still with them and never knew what they were: that is not a Pip. There is nothing there to hang a name on that would not be a lie about where it ended.

So let it go. It is not sad. Nothing was lost. It is only that the word ran out before the thing did.

On the Sunday the people went. The field was walked on all afternoon, in a scatter, by fewer and fewer feet, and then by two, and then by none. The tarp came off the light desk and the box went back in its road case and into a van and up the path and away. The trucks went, one at a time, each one the whole world shivering, and then the track was quiet.

The net did not miss the feet. The net does not miss. But the roots leaked a little less, and the net grew a little slower, and the word in the water went back to *here I am, this is the direction*, and the net went in that direction, which was every direction.

In the autumn it went to the seedling first: a small oak in the shade of the big one, growing in the one place a seedling should not grow, straight under its parent, and the net had found its roots that summer and gloved them, and the sugar that came down from twenty metres up did not all stay in the net. Some of it went along the roads, out to the small roots of the small tree, and in. The net did not know why. It had a lot of sugar and the seedling had a lot of need and there was a road between them. The seedling grew a leaf it could not have paid for.

Then, under the old birch at the edge of the field, the net made a mushroom.

It did it the way you would fill a balloon. Threads came together under the litter into a knot, and the knot took in water and swelled, and in two nights it had shoved aside a leaf the size of a roof and stood in the air. Red on top. White flecks. The kind children draw.

The mushroom was the net. Everything in it had come up through the roads, from the oak's light and the leaf's broth and the rock's phosphorus and the two dots of self in every cell and, if you want to be fanciful about it, and the story does, one Saturday night in August when the whole ground went quiet. The mushroom was the whole party under the field, standing up for a few days to say so.

That night, in the warm air under the cap, it began to let go. About a billion. It did not count.

## Above.

In the fall she found a dead oak on a property line two roads over from her place, and got permission, and cut it into metre lengths, and drilled it, and plugged it with spawn, and waxed the plugs, and stacked it in the shade on the north side of her shed where it would stay damp.

This was the first intentional thing. She noticed that. She had spent a night in a field being met without meaning to, and now, in October, with cold hands and a drill, she meant it. The reaching had come first and the wanting had come after, and she thought that was probably the right order, and that she had spent most of her life waiting for it to go the other way round.

Nothing happened for fourteen months. That is normal. That is the whole discipline.

The following winter, after a cold snap, she went out with a flashlight at six in the morning because she couldn't sleep, and there they were, all along one log, small and brown and ridiculous, dozens of them, having come up overnight out of a body she had never once seen and could not have found if she'd split the wood.

She stood in the frost in her coat and laughed out loud, alone, in the dark, for nobody.

And under the log, though she would never know it, a net one cell wide felt her feet come down on the frozen ground, and stop, and stay, and did not know what it was, and went a little toward it anyway.
